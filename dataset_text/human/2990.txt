Those words come from Greek , and were originally spelled with the Greek letter Φ -- it 's name was ( and still is ) " phi " . And originally in Greek , this was an aspirated Π -- " pi " . We have an aspirated " P " in English , although we do n't have a special letter for it -- in fact , most of our Ps are aspirated . Here 's a test you can do : hold your hand just about two inches in front of your mouth . Now say " Bob " . You can probably feel a faint puff of air on your hand when you say the " B " . Now say " pop " : this time , the puff of air on your hand should be a lot stronger . That 's aspiration . So originally , the Greek letter pi represented an unaspirated P -- without the strong puff of air ( this sound does n't normally exist in English ) -- and phi was an aspirated P -- with the strong puff of air . But then something happened . To make the " P " sound , what we do is first close our lips and allow the air inside the mouth to build up ; then we let the air out in a sort of explosion ( in phonetics this type of sound is called a " plosive " or a " stop " ) . Now try saying " P " , but do n't let the air build up in the mouth : let it out slowly . If you do it right , you 'll get a sound that is very much like an " F " sound ( the technical term for this is a " bilabial fricative " ) . And then all you have to do is to bring your bottom lip back slightly to touch your upper teeth , and you 're now actually saying " F " . And that is the history of " PH " .
