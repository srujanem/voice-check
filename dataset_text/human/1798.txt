Nobody can give a definitive answer to that question , for the reasons that [ HannasAnarion points out in a comment ] ( URL_2 ) . With human languages we can only look back some 6,000 - 10,000 years in the past , but humans have been speaking at least 35,000 years . What I 'll say below is heavily based on [ my answer to a similar thread from some months ago ] ( URL_5 ) . One thing to keep in mind is that most familiar European languages like German , French and Spanish descend from languages that have gender as far back as we can trace them . The farthest we can trace them is to the [ Proto - Indo - European laguage ] ( URL_3 ) , the reconstructed common ancestor of German , French , Spanish , English , Greek , Russian , Hindi , Armenian , Farsi , Albanian , Kurdish , Lithuanian and a bunch of other languages . Linguists are not sure exactly when it was spoken , but hypotheses range from 6,000 to 12,000 years ago . But one thing we know for sure is that Proto - Indo - European had gender , and the German , French and Spanish gender systems are modifications of Proto - Indo - European gender . English is a bit odd among Indo - European languages for having lost its gender system — and that happened less than 1,000 years ago . So the cheap answer to why German , French and Spanish have genders basically boils down to ( a ) they descend from a language that already had gender , ( b ) they have n't lost it in at least 6,000 years , ( c ) we just do n't know why Proto - Indo - European had gender , and in fact ( d ) we * ca n't * know because it 's prehistory . But there 's another way we can look at this question : by examining languages that * do n't * have grammatical gender , but have grammatical rules that are somewhat similar to it . One useful example is Japanese , which does n't have gender , but it does have one grammatical feature that 's very interesting in this context : [ noun classifiers ] ( URL_0 ) ( [ see also here ] ( URL_4 ) ) . Basically , in Japanese , any countable noun phrase requires the use of a * * count word * * or * * classifier * * , and which one is used depends highly on the noun . So you use a different count word depending on whether you 're counting sheep , cars , pencils , etc . At this point you should be struck by the similarity to grammatical gender . In fact , one of the theories about how gender systems develop in the histories of languages is that they start out as classifiers or something similar , and over time the words get " smushed together " and what was once a separate count word becomes a prefix or suffix to the noun . Linguists call this sort of process [ grammaticalization ] ( URL_1 ) . One familiar example ( though not about gender ) is that in English we have these three : 1 . I 'm going to let you finish . 2 . I 'm gon na let you finish . 3 . I 'm a let you finish . * I 'm going to X * , which started out with the literal meaning of moving from one place to another , is grammaticalizing into a future tense , and in the process it 's getting smushed together into * I 'm a * . So over thousands of years , you could picture Japanese going through a combination of changes like these : * A lot of the count words become archaic and eventually disappear from the language . ( This has already happened to a bunch of them ! ) * Count words start showing up redundantly on both adjectives and nouns in the same sentence . * The surviving classifiers get thoroughly smushed into the nouns and adjectives , and become prefixes or suffixes . And voilà , grammatical gender ! Disclaimer / clarification : the above is * one * theory of how * some * languages may have acquired grammatical gender . Either of these could be possible : ( a ) the theory is flat - out wrong , ( b ) the theory is true for * some * languages but not for others ( there may be more than one way that grammatical gender systems emerge ) .
