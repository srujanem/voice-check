All radioactive elements have a certain half life , the time it will take for half of the sample to decay into another stable ( non radioactive ) element / isotope . By measuring how much of the sample is left and compare it to how much they would expect in a living sample , they can then calculate the time it has been decaying ( without replenishment ) , and this gives an age . Each decay mechanism ( one element to another , with its specific half life ) is called a radiological clock . Multiple clocks will be used for accuracy . I 'm not sure how specific accuracies are calculated , but obviously clocks with larger half lives allow you to date older things while sacrificing granularity ( precision ) . As far as verification goes , the values measured will be released , so you can rerun the maths and see it checks out . However if they were tampering with it they would simply change the values measured themselves , so short of obtaining a sample ( and equipment ) and measuring it yourself I 'm not sure what you could do .
