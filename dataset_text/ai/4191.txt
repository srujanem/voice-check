It's normal to remember disturbing or emotionally significant events more vividly than other memories. This is because the brain pays more attention to and stores more information about events that are emotionally charged or meaningful in some way. When we experience strong emotions, our brain releases chemicals called hormones that can help to strengthen the connections between brain cells, making it easier to remember the event. This process is known as emotional memory consolidation.\n\nEmotional memories tend to be more vivid and longer lasting than other types of memories because they are processed differently in the brain. When you experience a strong emotion, the amygdala, a small almond-shaped structure in the brain, becomes more active and helps to encode the memory more strongly. The hippocampus, another part of the brain that is important for memory, also plays a role in helping us to remember emotionally significant events.\n\nSo, in a way, our ability to remember disturbing or emotionally significant events does involve chemistry, because the release of hormones like adrenaline and cortisol can affect how the brain processes and stores memories. However, it's not just chemistry that determines why we remember certain things and not others. There are many other factors that can influence how well we remember something, including how much attention we paid to the event at the time it happened, how much the event stood out or was unusual, and how much we think about the event after it happened.
