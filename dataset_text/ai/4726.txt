Stars twinkle because they are so far away that their light has to pass through the Earth's atmosphere before it reaches us. The atmosphere is made up of gases and particles, and as the light travels through it, it can be affected by things like temperature, humidity, and wind. These changes in the atmosphere can cause the light from the star to bend and shift as it travels, which makes the star appear to twinkle or shimmer.\n\nPlanets, on the other hand, are much closer to us than stars, so their light does not have to pass through as much of the Earth's atmosphere before it reaches us. This means that the light from planets is less likely to be affected by the atmosphere, and so they do not twinkle like stars do.\n\nAs for why some faint stars seem to disappear when we look directly at them, but can be noticed when we look at a nearby patch of sky, this is because our eyes are not very good at detecting faint objects that are surrounded by a lot of other bright objects. When we look directly at a faint star, our eyes are drawn to the brighter objects around it, which makes it harder to see the faint star. However, if we look at a patch of sky near the faint star, our eyes are not as overwhelmed by the brightness of the other objects, and so we are able to see the faint star more easily.
