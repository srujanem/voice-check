Carbon dating and radiometric dating are methods used to determine the age of objects by measuring the amount of a particular type of isotope they contain. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with a different number of neutrons. In carbon dating, scientists measure the amount of carbon-14, a radioactive isotope of carbon, in a sample. Carbon-14 is present in all living things in a constant amount while they are alive, but when they die, they stop absorbing carbon-14 and the amount of carbon-14 begins to decrease through a process called radioactive decay. By measuring the amount of carbon-14 in a sample and comparing it to the known decay rate of carbon-14, scientists can determine how old the sample is. In radiometric dating, scientists measure the amount of a particular isotope in a sample and compare it to the amount of the isotope's decay products. The ratio of the isotope to its decay products tells scientists how old the sample is. To determine the initial amount of the isotope in the sample, scientists use a technique called isotope dilution. This involves adding a known amount of the isotope to the sample and measuring the resulting increase in the ratio of the isotope to its decay products. From this, scientists can calculate the initial amount of the isotope in the sample.
