The possession of nuclear weapons is regulated by international law, specifically by the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT). The NPT is a treaty that was signed in 1968 and has been ratified by nearly all countries in the world. The NPT divides countries into two categories: nuclear-weapon states and non-nuclear-weapon states.\n\nNuclear-weapon states are countries that had nuclear weapons before the NPT was signed, or that have developed nuclear weapons since then. These countries are the United States, Russia, China, France, and the United Kingdom. These five countries are allowed to possess nuclear weapons under the NPT, but they are also required to work towards disarmament, which means reducing the number of nuclear weapons they have.\n\nNon-nuclear-weapon states are countries that do not have nuclear weapons and have agreed not to develop or acquire them. These countries are not allowed to have nuclear weapons under the NPT. If a non-nuclear-weapon state is found to be developing nuclear weapons, it can be punished by other countries through economic sanctions or other measures.\n\nThe NPT is an important part of international law because it helps to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons to more countries, which can make the world a safer place. However, not all countries have signed the NPT, and some countries that have signed the treaty have not always followed its rules. This can cause problems and tensions between countries.
