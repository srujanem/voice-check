Mathematicians often discover new formulas through a combination of experimentation, observation, and theoretical reasoning. Here's a simplified explanation of the process:\n\nMathematicians start with a problem they want to solve. For example, they might want to figure out how to calculate the area of a circle.\n\nThey try different approaches to solving the problem, using what they already know about math to guide them.\n\nAs they try different approaches, they might notice patterns or relationships between different quantities. For example, they might notice that the area of a circle is always a certain number times the radius of the circle, squared.\n\nMathematicians use these patterns and relationships to come up with a formula that can be used to solve the problem. For example, the formula for the area of a circle is: A = πr^2, where A is the area of the circle, r is the radius of the circle, and π is a special number that is approximately 3.14.\n\nMathematicians test their formula to make sure it works for different cases and situations. If it works well, they might publish their findings and share their formula with other mathematicians.\n\nSo, in a sense, discovering new formulas is a little bit like guessing and testing different approaches until something works. However, it also involves careful observation and a lot of thinking and reasoning about the underlying principles and patterns of math.
