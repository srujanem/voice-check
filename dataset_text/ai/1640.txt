Game theory is a way of thinking about and analyzing situations in which people or organizations have conflicting interests and make decisions that affect each other. It's often used to understand how people and organizations behave in different situations, such as when they're competing for resources or trying to cooperate with each other. A good metaphor for understanding game theory is a game of chess. In chess, each player has their own pieces and their own goals, and they make decisions about where to move their pieces based on what they think their opponent is going to do. Just like in real life, the players in chess have to think about what the other player might do, and try to outsmart them in order to win. The prisoner's dilemma is a specific example of a game in game theory. In the prisoner's dilemma, two people have been arrested and are being held in separate cells. The police have enough evidence to charge them with a crime, but they don't have enough evidence to convict them. The police offer each prisoner a deal: if one prisoner confesses and the other doesn't, the confessing prisoner will go free and the other prisoner will go to prison for a long time. If both prisoners confess, they'll both go to prison for a shorter time. If neither prisoner confesses, they'll both go to prison for an even shorter time. The dilemma for the prisoners is that if they both confess, they'll both end up going to prison for a shorter time than if one of them confesses and the other doesn't. However, if one prisoner confesses and the other doesn't, the confessing prisoner will go free and the other prisoner will go to prison for a long time.
