When someone is summoned to testify before Congress, they are usually required to do so under oath, which means that they are promising to tell the truth. If someone lies while under oath, they can be charged with perjury, which is a crime. So, there is an incentive for someone who is testifying to provide honest and accurate answers to the questions they are asked.\n\nHowever, it is also possible for someone to refuse to answer certain questions if they believe that answering the question would violate their constitutional rights, such as their right to free speech or their right against self-incrimination. In these cases, the person may invoke their Fifth Amendment rights and refuse to answer the question.\n\nIf someone gives deliberately vague or meaningless answers, they may be seen as not fully cooperating with the Congressional investigation and could face consequences such as being held in contempt of Congress. This means that they could be fined or even imprisoned for not cooperating with the investigation.
