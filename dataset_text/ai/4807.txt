Fractional reserve banking and Ponzi schemes are two different financial systems or practices that have some similarities, but are also quite different in important ways.\n\nFractional reserve banking is a system that banks use to manage deposits and loans. Under this system, banks are required to keep a certain percentage of their deposits on hand as reserves, while they can lend out the rest of the money to borrowers. For example, if a bank has $100 in deposits and is required to keep 10% as reserves, it can lend out $90 to borrowers. The idea behind fractional reserve banking is to allow banks to make more loans and earn more money from interest, while still being able to meet the demands of depositors who might want to withdraw their money at any time.\n\nA Ponzi scheme, on the other hand, is a fraudulent investment scheme in which earlier investors are paid with the investments of more recent investors, rather than with actual profits earned. In a Ponzi scheme, the person running the scheme (the "operator") promises high returns to investors, but instead of using the investment money to generate profits, the operator simply takes the money and uses it to pay earlier investors. This creates the illusion of a profitable investment, and can continue for a while as long as more and more people are willing to invest. However, eventually the scheme collapses because there is not enough money to pay all the investors, and the operator usually ends up disappearing with the remaining funds.\n\nSo, while both fractional reserve banking and Ponzi schemes involve the use of other people's money to generate profits, fractional reserve banking is a legitimate and regulated financial system that is intended to be transparent and fair, while Ponzi schemes are fraudulent and deceitful, and are designed to take advantage of people's trust and greed.
