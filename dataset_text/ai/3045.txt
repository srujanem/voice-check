The Coriolis effect is a phenomenon that occurs when a moving object is viewed from a rotating frame of reference. It is named after French mathematician and physicist Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who first described the effect in 1835. Imagine you are standing on a spinning top. If you throw a ball straight ahead, it will seem to curve to the right (if you are in the Northern Hemisphere) or to the left (if you are in the Southern Hemisphere). This is because the top is spinning and you are moving with it, so the ball appears to curve as it travels. Now imagine that you are standing on the Earth, which is also a spinning top (though a much bigger one). The Earth spins on its axis from west to east, and this causes the Coriolis effect. The Coriolis effect is most noticeable in the movement of air and water. For example, when air or water moves from high pressure to low pressure, it will seem to curve as it travels. This is why winds and ocean currents tend to curve rather than move in a straight line. The Coriolis effect is a very small force, and it is usually only noticeable over long distances or long periods of time. It is not strong enough to cause objects to curve sharply, but it can have a noticeable influence on the paths of things like airplanes, missiles, and storms.
