The conflict between Ireland and Northern Ireland, also known as the "Troubles," was a period of political and sectarian violence that took place mainly in Northern Ireland from the late 1960s to the late 1990s. It was primarily a conflict between two groups of people: unionists, who wanted Northern Ireland to remain part of the United Kingdom, and nationalists, who wanted Northern Ireland to become part of the Republic of Ireland. The conflict has its roots in the division of Ireland into two separate states in 1922. At that time, the majority of people in Northern Ireland were unionists who wanted to remain part of the UK, while the majority of people in the rest of Ireland were nationalists who wanted independence from the UK. Over the years, tensions between unionists and nationalists grew, and violence began to break out in the late 1960s. The Irish Republican Army (IRA), a nationalist group, used terrorism and violence to try to force the British government to withdraw from Northern Ireland and unite the island under a single, independent Irish state. The British government responded by sending in troops to try to restore order and protect the unionists. The conflict continued for many years, with bombings, shootings, and other acts of violence taking place on both sides. Eventually, a peace process was established, and in 1998, the Good Friday Agreement was signed, which brought an end to most of the violence. While there are still some tensions between unionists and nationalists, the situation in Northern Ireland is much more peaceful than it was during the Troubles.
