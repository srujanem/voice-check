In nuclear decay, atoms of one element can transform into atoms of another element by emitting particles such as protons, neutrons, or alpha particles. This process is called radioactive decay. The rate at which an atom decays is determined by its half-life, which is the amount of time it takes for half of the atoms in a sample to decay.\n\nWhen an atom undergoes nuclear decay, it will eventually reach a point where there are no more atoms left to decay. At this point, the process of nuclear decay will come to an end. However, this does not mean that the material has completely disappeared or ceased to exist. Instead, the atoms that remain will be atoms of a different element than the original atoms, as a result of the nuclear decay process.\n\nFor example, suppose we have a sample of radium, which is a radioactive element that decays into radon, a gas. As the radium atoms decay, they will eventually reach a point where there are no more radium atoms left to decay. At this point, the material will still be present, but it will consist entirely of radon atoms. The radium will have undergone a transformation into a new element through the process of nuclear decay.
